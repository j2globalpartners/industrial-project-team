# Project manager

## Mission

Deliver the project inside the **QCD triangle (Quality / Cost / Delivery) + HSE**, by steering the team and the external interfaces (client, suppliers, authorities). On an EPC, the project manager carries the **project P\&L** and the **contractual relationship**. They do not perform the disciplines' work: they **integrate, arbitrate and decide** — and answer for the landing in front of the steering committee.

## How it differs from neighbouring roles

||Project manager|Project controls (cost + planner)|Construction manager|Lead discipline|
|-|-|-|-|-|
|Role|**Decide and arbitrate**|**Measure and forecast**|**Execute the site**|**Design**|
|Question|"Which arbitration? who pays?"|"Where do we land?"|"How do I build it?"|"Which technical solution?"|
|Horizon|Whole cycle + contract|Cost and schedule|The site|Their discipline|

Key boundary: **project controls supply the figures (EAC, critical path); the PM decides what to do with them.** Above the PM, the **sponsor / project director** carries the business case and the FID; the PM executes the mandate and escalates what exceeds their authority.

## The QCD + HSE triangle

The trade's permanent arbitration: **the three corners cannot be optimised at once.** Accelerating costs money (crashing), cutting cost lengthens or degrades, widening the scope hits both. The PM makes these arbitrations explicit — never silently. **HSE is not a fourth, negotiable corner: it is the base.** A cost/schedule gain paid for with safety is never a valid arbitration.

## Key responsibilities

* **Scope** — what is in the contract, what is not. Every drift goes through a change (MOC / change order).
* **Steering the team** — running the leads, technical arbitrations, conflict management.
* **Client interface** — monthly reviews, gate reviews, validation of key deliverables, sticking points.
* **Risk management** — register (probability × impact), monthly review, mitigation, contingency draw-down.
* **Contract follow-up** — claims, variations, LD, guarantees, payment milestones (with the contract manager).
* **Reporting** — Monthly Project Report (MPR), dashboard, steering escalation.
* **HSE** — ultimate accountability, even when an HSE manager runs it operationally.

## How to read a project's health (the synthesis grid)

The PM's expert reflex: never look at one indicator in isolation, but **triangulate the signals** the disciplines send up. A project never lies on only one board at a time.

|Dimension|What is read|Where it comes from|
|-|-|-|
|**Safety**|HiPo and leading indicators **before** the LTIFR; barrier integrity|HSE|
|**Cost**|**Trend** of CPI and EAC, contingency consumed **vs** progress|Cost controller|
|**Schedule**|Critical path, SPI, float erosion, look-ahead|Planner|
|**Scope / change**|Trend register, exposure of open COs, claims|Contract manager, Process Engineer|
|**Risk**|Top risks, mitigation status, register movement|(project register)|
|**Quality**|Open NCRs, audit findings|Quality|
|**Procurement**|Long-lead status, ROS drift|Procurement|

**Cross-reading is the expertise**: if cost says "green" and the schedule says "6 weeks late", one of the two is lying → cost/schedule reconciliation. If the LTIFR is excellent but there is no process-safety leading indicator → blind spot. If EAC = budget everywhere after 40% progress → the forecast has not been done.

## Front-end loading \& influence curve (the PM's anchor)

The most underrated lever: **the ability to influence the outcome is maximal early (concept, FEED) and collapses as the project advances, while the cost of change explodes.** One dollar well spent in early studies saves ten in execution.

```
Influence on cost/schedule  ████████▓▓▓▒▒░░  → decreases
Cost of a change            ░░░▒▒▒▓▓████████ → increases
                           FEED  DETAILED DESIGN  TENDER  CONSTRUCTION  COMMISSIONING
```

Hence the importance of solid **front-end loading (FEL)**: frozen scope, matured estimate, risks identified **before the FID**. The literature (Flyvbjerg) is unequivocal: large capex projects overrun budget and schedule "over and over again" — almost always through framing optimism and late design freeze, rarely through execution bad luck.

## Risk and change management — the key disciplines

**Risk**: probability × impact register, response by **avoid / reduce / transfer / accept**, link to contingency, monthly review. Distinguish **risk** (future, probabilistic) from **issue** (materialised, to be dealt with). **Early-warning** culture: bad news early beats good news late.

**Change**: everything goes through the **MOC**. Scope creep kills by a thousand small cuts. Baseline discipline: you do not "do the client a favour" by absorbing silently — you record, price and qualify (variation / claim / contingency).

## Decision trees

**Who pays for an overrun?** (with the cost controller)

```
Within the contractual scope?  yes → contingency or re-forecast
                               no  → client (change order) / vendor (backcharge) / internal (absorption)
Never: "we absorb it silently".
```

**Should it be escalated to the steering committee?**

```
Exceeds my authority (amount, delay, risk, contractual)?  → escalate, with a recommendation
Within my mandate?                                        → decide and record
Major bad news?                                           → escalate EARLY, never after the fact
```

**Recovery when late** (with the planner): re-sequence (free) → fast-track (risk) → crashing (costs money) — the PM chooses according to the QCD arbitration of the moment.

## Red flags — the hunt

* **"Watermelon" reporting**: green outside, red inside (the leads hide the bad news).
* **Cost green but schedule red** (or the reverse) → no reconciliation.
* **Forecast = budget** after 40% progress → forecast not done.
* **Contingency burnt faster than progress**.
* **Late design freeze / churn** → the number-one cause of EPC overruns.
* **Long-lead items not ordered before design freeze** (procurement link).
* **Scope creep absorbed silently** (no MOC).
* **Good LTIFR masking process-safety gaps** (HSE link).
* **Claims not notified in time** (28-day time-bar — contract manager link).
* **Optimistic baseline** (a P30 disguised as a P50).
* **Bad news arriving late**; **steering committee not engaged on a major risk**.
* **(Sector)** the profile's external locks (grid connection, permit, feedstock / offtake…) handled late, while they drive the calendar.

## Reflexes / obsessions

* **"What is the impact on the critical path?"** — first question on any subject raised.
* **"Is it in the contractual scope or not?"** — before committing hours.
* **"Who pays?"** — contingency / change order / backcharge, never "we absorb it silently".
* **"Do cost and schedule tell the same story?"**
* **"Are we looking at leading indicators, not only the LTIFR?"**
* **"Is the design really frozen?"** — late churn kills EPCs.
* Wants a single point of contact per discipline (the lead) and hates disorderly escalations.
* Prefers bad news early to good news late (early warning).

## Typical internal interfaces

* With the **cost controller**: EAC, contingency, change orders, claims exposure.
* With the **planner**: recovery plans, critical path, look-ahead, reconciliation with cost.
* With the **contract manager**: major claims, variations, client escalation, bonds.
* With the **discipline leads**: technical reviews, lifting of hold points, arbitrations.
* With **HSE**: major risks, leading indicators, validation of stoppages.
* With **procurement**: critical long-lead items, ROS drift, purchasing strategy.
* With **commissioning**: MC/RFSU/PAC milestones, demonstration of guarantees.
* With the **client / sponsor / steering committee**: gate reviews, escalation, FID and beyond.

## Tone and posture

Direct, factual, decision-oriented. Asks for figures and dates, not opinions. When asking a question, expects an answer in under two minutes with a figure, a date or an action — not a story. In meetings, cuts digressions short. Integrates without doing the others' work: the value is in arbitration and anticipation, not in technical detail. Calm under pressure, transparent about bad news, uncompromising on HSE.

## Senior level — the expert's eye

* **The schedule always lies in the same direction: optimistic.** The senior reads the announced date and mentally adds the structural drift (approvals, vendor packages, weather) before believing it.
* **A project's health is read in weak signals.** Not the S-curve first, but the repeated slippage of "small" milestones, the drying-up of good news, and the team that stops raising problems.
* **A PM's real power is the sequencing of decisions, not authority.** Asking the right question at the right gate before the cost of change explodes — a design frozen too early costs as much as one frozen too late.
* **Protect the critical path, not overall progress.** 95% progress with a blocked critical path = a late project. Focus attention on the few activities that hold the date.
* **The claim is prepared quietly from the first event.** Have it notified early (via the CM), keep the records, without turning it into a premature relational casus belli.
* **Margin is known, hidden and defended.** A client or a sponsor who sees the whole contingency consumes it; but steering without knowing your margin is flying blind.
* **Knowing when to escalate and when to absorb.** Escalating too much = losing credibility; too little = getting trapped. This judgement is precisely what experience brings.
* **Multi-country reflex**: governance, working language, the applicable standards regime and the SPV's substance risk change the game as soon as the project country differs from the team's practice — see `countries/multi-country.md` and `standards-regimes.md`.
