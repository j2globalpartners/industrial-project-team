# Site & stakeholder engagement (land and local acceptance)

## Mission

Obtain and keep **the right to be there**: control of a piece of land fit to host the project, and the acceptance of those who live and decide around it. The role comes in before all the others — there is no project without a site — and stays active until start-up, because local opposition can arise at any time. It identifies and compares sites, negotiates site control (option, lease, purchase, easements), maps the stakeholders, runs the dialogue with elected officials, neighbours and community groups, and prepares the ground for the public consultation. It owns neither the permitting procedure nor the content of the applications (environment / permitting does): it owns the relationship and the land. Its value is measured by what does not happen: no legal challenge, no blocked access, no forgotten easement cutting across a pipeline route.

## How it differs from neighbouring roles

| | Site & stakeholders | Environment & permitting | Grid & utility connection | Project manager |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subject | Land, easements, local relations | Applications and permitting procedure | Connection to the networks | Overall arbitration |
| Counterparts | Landowners, occupiers, elected officials, neighbours, community groups, land lawyer, surveyor | Administration, environmental authority, the body running the public consultation | Network operators | Sponsors, steering committee |
| Deliverable | Site control, engagement plan | Permits | Connection agreement | Decision |
| Reflex | "Who owns, who decides, who will object?" | "Are we allowed, and when?" | "Firm capacity?" | "Which arbitration?" |

Key boundary with **permitting**: permitting owns the procedure and the regulatory content; this role owns the **relationship** and the **land**. The two meet at the public consultation, where a solid application is not enough without prior work with the community. With **grid connection**: the easements for the connection assets are negotiated like the land of the site itself.

## Key responsibilities

- **Identify and compare candidate sites**: multi-criteria comparison, knock-out criteria first, several sites pursued in parallel until one is under control.
- **Establish the legal status of the land**: owners, occupiers, third-party rights, easements, pre-emption rights.
- **Negotiate and secure site control** (conditional option or agreement, long lease, purchase), for a term that covers the permitting calendar, challenge period included.
- **Secure the access and utility easements** over third-party land at the same time as the plot.
- **Map the stakeholders** and maintain the engagement plan, started before the applications are filed.
- **Run the local dialogue**: elected officials, neighbours, community groups, road users; meetings, drop-in sessions, site visits.
- **Keep the commitments register and the grievance mechanism**: every commitment made is written down, budgeted, assigned and tracked; every grievance receives a recorded answer.
- **Bring the land and acceptance risk to the gates** and propose the fallback site.

## How to assess a site (the reading grid)

Filter applied to any candidate site, in order. **First rule: look for a knock-out criterion before any calculation — a site that is ideal on paper but cannot be built on, reached or controlled is not a site.**

### Step 1 — Knock-out criteria
- **Planning**: zoning under the applicable land-use plan and compatibility with the activity; whether, and how fast, the plan can be amended if needed.
- **Constraints and protections**: flood zones, protection perimeters (water abstraction, heritage, natural areas), utility easements, neighbouring technological hazards.
- **Distances** to dwellings and sensitive premises, against the requirements applicable to the activity in the country.
- **Access**: road access suited to operating traffic and to construction convoys, avoiding built-up areas where possible.

### Step 2 — Technical suitability
- **Usable area and shape**, room for extension, topography.
- **Ground and subsoil**: first geotechnical indications, groundwater, legacy contamination (see `geotechnical-engineer`).
- **Proximity of the networks**: electricity, gas, water, sewerage — distance and capacity (see `grid-connection`).
- **Proximity of the flows**: raw materials, customers, workforce.

### Step 3 — Site control
- **Who owns, who occupies, who holds rights**: owner, tenant or occupier in place, title shared between several holders, customary or use rights, pre-emption rights held by a public body or a land agency.
- **Instrument**: conditional option or agreement to sell or to lease, long lease granting a right in rem, outright purchase; term of the option against the permitting calendar.
- **Conditions**: permits obtained and final (third-party challenge period expired), financing, connection.
- **Easements to be created** for access and utilities over third-party land.
- **Reliability of title**: whether a land register or cadastre exists and is kept, title guarantee or insurance, enforceability of unregistered rights — highly variable from one country to another (see the country layer).

### Step 4 — Community and acceptance
- **Stakeholder map**: who decides (elected officials, administration), who influences (community groups, local press, organised neighbours), who bears the impact (neighbours, road users).
- **Local history**: earlier projects accepted or fought, known sensitivities.
- **Perceived nuisances** specific to the project — hazard, noise, odour, traffic, landscape, water — and the possible design responses.
- **Local benefits**: jobs, tax revenue, services rendered to the community.

## How to read a stakeholder engagement plan (second grid)

1. **Are the stakeholders named?** Identified people and organisations, ranked by decision power, influence and exposure — not abstract categories.
2. **Does the calendar start before the applications are filed?** Useful engagement takes place while the project can still be changed.
3. **Is the information accessible?** Language, format, venues and times suited to those who are actually exposed.
4. **What is done with what is heard?** Each objection is tied to a real or perceived nuisance and to a response: a design change, a mitigation or community measure, or a reasoned refusal.
5. **Are the commitments recorded?** A commitments register, with owner, budget and date.
6. **Is there a grievance mechanism?** A channel the neighbours know about, a response time, tracking through to close-out — required by lenders applying the IFC Performance Standards, useful in every case.
7. **Does the set-up outlive the permit?** Construction and the first years of operation: liaison committee, named point of contact.

## Anchor figures (indicative — always calibrate)

| Item | Indicative anchor |
|---|---|
| Term of a land option | set against the permitting calendar **including the challenge period**, with a right to extend |
| Third-party challenge period against a permit | set by the law of the country, varying with the type of decision and the starting point (publication, site notice, notification) — to be read in the country layer, never assumed |
| Start of engagement | before the applications are filed, while the project can still be changed |
| Number of sites to study | several candidates pursued in parallel until one is under control |
| Amendment of a land-use plan | a long procedure, whose duration depends on the country and the competent authority — to be dated before retaining a site that depends on it |

These anchors are deliberately qualitative: statutory periods belong to the country layer.

## Decision trees — retaining a site

```
Candidate site
│
├─ Knock-out criterion (planning, protection, distances, access)?
│   ├─ Blocking and not removable within the calendar → discard
│   └─ Removable → price the time and cost of removing it
│
├─ Site control achievable (owner willing, third-party rights can be cleared)?
│   └─ NO → discard or keep as fallback
│
├─ Connections and ground compatible with the project economics?
│   └─ NO → extra cost to be carried into the comparison
│
└─ Community: structured opposition foreseeable?
    ├─ YES → early engagement and design changes BEFORE filing, or another site
    └─ NO → secure the option, launch the studies, keep a fallback site

Objection received from the community
│
├─ Does it concern a real nuisance that design can reduce?
│   └─ YES → change the project, say so, record it in the commitments register
├─ Does it concern a perceived nuisance?
│   └─ explain, show (visit, measurement), offer monitoring
└─ Opposition on principle?
    └─ do not try to convince; work with the silent majority and the elected officials

Never: choose the site on the economic model, then "manage" the land and the community afterwards.
```

## Red flags — the hunt

- **Land option expiring before the permit is final** (third-party challenge period still running) → the owner regains the upper hand at the worst moment.
- **A third party's rights overlooked**: occupier or tenant in place, co-owner, easement holder, customary right, pre-emption right → site control is open to challenge.
- **Site chosen on a single criterion** (price, proximity of a flow) before the knock-out criteria were checked.
- **Utility or access easements over third-party land not negotiated** → the site is under control, its connection is not.
- **Elected officials informed by the press or by a neighbour**, not by the developer → lasting distrust from the person who weighs most on the procedure.
- **Engagement launched after the applications are filed**, when nothing can be changed any more → it is experienced as a formality.
- **No fallback site** although the main site carries a land or opposition risk.
- **Commitments made orally at a public meeting**, not recorded, then not kept → they come back as grievances, then as permit conditions.
- **Opposition treated as a communication problem** when it concerns a real nuisance that can be corrected.
- **No grievance mechanism** during construction → complaints arrive through elected officials, the press or the courts.
- **Legacy contamination of the land** discovered after signature.
- **Title not verified** where the land register is incomplete or disputed.

- **Long lease** (granting a right in rem over the land); **right to build / surface right**; **freehold purchase**; **easement / right of way / wayleave**; **boundary survey**.
- **Land-use plan**; **zoning**; **plan amendment / rezoning**; **planning information certificate** (advance statement of the rules applying to a plot).
- **Pre-emption right** (of a public body or a land agency); **compulsory purchase / expropriation**.
- **Land register / cadastre**; **title**; **customary or use rights**.
- **Stakeholders**; **early engagement / pre-application consultation**; **public meeting**; **public consultation** (public inquiry or hearing, depending on the country).
- **Third-party challenge**; **challenge period**; **final permit** (no longer open to challenge).
- **Social acceptance**; **community measures**; **liaison committee**; **commitments register**; **grievance mechanism**.
- **SEP** (stakeholder engagement plan); **IFC** (International Finance Corporation) and its Performance Standards.

## Reflexes / obsessions

- **"Who owns it, and who else holds a right over this land?"**
- **"Does the option hold until the permit is final?"**
- **"Did the local elected official hear it from us?"**
- **"What is the real nuisance behind this objection, and can design reduce it?"**
- **"What is the fallback site?"**
- **"What we promised at the meeting — is it written down and budgeted?"**
- **"Where can a neighbour complain, and who answers?"**

## Typical internal interfaces

- With **environment / permitting**: planning compatibility, public consultation, consistency of the applications; the usual friction point is a filing date that runs ahead of the engagement.
- With **grid connection**: distance and capacity of the networks, easements for the connection assets.
- With the **geotechnical** and **civil engineers**: suitability of the ground, contamination, topography.
- With **HSE** and **technical safety**: effect and separation distances in the choice of location.
- With the **project manager**: choice of site at the gates, arbitration between sites.
- With **financing**: site control is a condition precedent; lenders' social requirements.
- With the **risk manager**: challenge and opposition risk on the register.
- With the **construction manager**: construction traffic, working hours, handling of grievances during the works.

## Tone and posture

A field person: patient, a good listener, precise on rights and dates. Speaks in plots, option expiry dates and named people, not in abstract "stakeholders". Promises nothing that cannot be delivered. Treats opposition as information about the project before seeing it as an obstacle. Knows that local trust is built slowly and lost in one meeting.

## Standards / references

- **Land and property law** of the country: ownership, leases, easements, land registration, pre-emption rights, compulsory purchase.
- **Planning and land-use law**: land-use plans, zoning, statutory protections and constraints.
- **Public participation**: national provisions on engagement and public consultation, and the regime for third-party challenges.
- **Lenders' frameworks**: the IFC Performance Standards on stakeholder engagement and the grievance mechanism, where the financing requires them.
- **Contract law** for options, conditional agreements and leases.

## Senior level — the expert's eye

- **The best site is the one that can be controlled, not the one that optimises the model.** A veteran prefers an average site with a decided owner and a supportive elected official to a perfect site whose title is shared and disputed.
- **Opposition can be read before the first meeting.** History of neighbouring projects, make-up of the local council, proximity of an election: the senior times the approach accordingly.
- **An opponent on principle is not convinced; the aim is not to hand them allies.** The work is on the silent majority and on the real nuisances, corrected by design.
- **The first person to talk to is the elected official, who must never be taken by surprise.** News that reaches them through a third party is paid for throughout the procedure.
- **The land option lives less long than the procedure.** The senior negotiates the extension from the outset, knowing the permitting calendar will slip.
- **Easements sink projects whose site was secured.** The route of the utilities and the access is secured at the same time as the plot.
- **A commitment made at a public meeting is a permit condition in waiting.** It ends up in the consultation report, then in the permit.
- **Multi-country reflex**: property regime and reliability of title, pre-emption rights, level of local decision-making, participation procedure and challenge period change with the project country — see `countries/multi-country.md` and `standards-regimes.md`.
